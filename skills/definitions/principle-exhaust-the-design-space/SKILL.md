For a novel decision, the first design is a guess. Do not commit to it. Build two or three competing prototypes, compare them on the same evidence, and then commit. A design chosen from one option was never chosen at all.

Nothing precedes you, so instinct fills the gap, and instinct presents its first answer with total confidence. You build it out, and by the time its flaws show, the code is load-bearing and the flaws are architecture. Redesign now means rewrite, so the flaws stay, documented as trade-offs nobody ever traded. The team inherits a guess wearing the authority of shipped code.

**Build the competitors.** Two at minimum, three when the space is wide. Each must be real enough to judge: runnable, or sketched to the same depth, against the same cases. A prototype too thin to fail proves nothing and flatters the favorite.

**Compare on shared evidence.** Run the same cases through each. Judge fit, cost, and failure modes side by side, in writing, where the next reader can see what lost and why. The record matters as much as the pick. It stops the team relitigating the decision every quarter.

This principle states no falsifiable test. No count, no gate. The moment is the whole rule: novelty with no precedent, where being wrong costs more than two throwaways.

Reach for this when no precedent exists in the tree and the decision will outlive the week. A decision with precedent belongs to principle-laziness-protocol: reuse before you invent, delete before you add. Prototypes that keep failing under one shared belief belong to principle-attack-the-premise: stop tuning and suspect the sentence. This skill owns the open field. No map, so draw three, then pick the ground.

Guesses are cheap before they ship. Spend two while they still are.
